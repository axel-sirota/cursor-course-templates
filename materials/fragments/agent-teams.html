9. Agent Teams: Coordinated Parallel Work

Everything in this module so far follows one shape: a main session that farms work out and collects results. Subagents research and implement, worktrees keep them from stepping on each other, hooks keep the contract honest, but the workers never speak to one another. Agent teams change that shape. A team is a group of full Claude Code sessions that share a task list, message each other directly, and coordinate under a lead. Teams launched alongside Opus 4.6 (February 2026), but they are not tied to any model: the official guidance in fact recommends Sonnet for teammates, and everything in this section works that way.

Teams are experimental and off by default. Enable them with an environment variable in your project’s .claude/settings.json: 

{
  "env": {
    "CLAUDE_CODE_EXPERIMENTAL_AGENT_TEAMS": "1"
  }
}

The four moving parts

A team has exactly four pieces:[4]

  • Team lead: your session. It breaks the goal into tasks, spawns teammates, arbitrates disagreements, and synthesizes the result.
  • Teammates: full, independent Claude Code sessions, not lightweight subagents. Each has its own context window and works through tasks on its own.
  • Shared task list: the coordination backbone. Teammates claim tasks with file locking, so two teammates can never grab the same one. Tasks can declare dependencies; when a blocking task completes, its dependents unblock automatically.
  • Mailbox: peer-to-peer messages. Teammates can question each other’s decisions, share findings, and negotiate interfaces without routing everything through the lead.

Teammates can be spawned from the subagent definitions you already wrote in the Subagent Architecture section: mention the agent by name when you ask the lead to build the team. The definition’s tools and model fields are honored; its skills and mcpServers fields are not: teammates do not inherit those, so anything a teammate needs from a skill has to go into the task description instead.

Two control mechanisms keep a team from running away from you. First, the plan-approval flow: you can require that a teammate present its plan and get the lead’s approval before touching code. Use it for any task you would not hand to a new hire without a design review. Second, quality-gate hooks: three team events are hookable, and an exit code of 2 pushes the work back instead of letting it through.

Hook eventFires whenExit 2 means
TeammateIdleA teammate runs out of claimed workSend it back for more (e.g. its tests do not pass yet)
TaskCreatedA new task lands on the shared listReject the task (e.g. it is missing acceptance criteria)
TaskCompletedA teammate marks a task doneReopen the task (e.g. the contract validator fails)

A TaskCompleted gate that reruns the service tests looks just like the contract hooks from earlier in this module: same JSON shape, new event name, and exit 2 instead of a block message. You will wire one up in the Hard tier of Lab 8.

Subagents or a team?

This is the decision the whole module has been building toward. The two models are not rivals: they answer different questions.

SubagentsAgent team
Who coordinates The main session orchestrates everything itself The lead sets direction; teammates self-claim from the shared task list
Do workers talk to each other No: each reports back to the caller only Yes: peer-to-peer messages through the mailbox
Token cost Lower: only condensed results return to your context High: every teammate is a full session running in parallel
Best for Parallel research and well-scoped tasks where only the result matters Work that needs discussion between workers: cross-service changes, reviews that challenge each other
Teams do not isolate teammates in worktrees

Every teammate shares the working directory. Two teammates editing the same file will corrupt each other’s work exactly like two developers editing one checkout. The fix is partitioning, not isolation: give each teammate ownership of a different service’s files and say so in the task descriptions. Worktrees are the subagent story; teams are the coordination story. Do not expect one to do the other’s job.

Cost: a team is roughly 7× a single session

Every teammate burns tokens like a full session, so a team multiplies your spend by roughly the team size plus coordination overhead, about 7× for a typical setup.[8] Keep teams at 3–5 teammates, run teammates on Sonnet, and shut the team down as soon as the work lands.

Live-training note: a classroom of concurrent teams needs a generous tokens-per-minute limit. Coordinate with your org admin before the session. That is why the instructor runs Demo 8 while you run the scaled-down lab.

Known limitations

All documented, all real:[4]

  • No session resume while in-process teammates are running: finish or shut down the team first.
  • Task status can lag reality: nudge the lead to refresh if the list looks stale.
  • The lead may stop early with tasks still open: tell it to keep going.
  • One team per session: you cannot run two teams side by side.
  • No nested teams: a teammate cannot spawn a team of its own.

Demo 8: a real team, instructor-driven

Instructor runs this (token-heavy)

This demo runs three Sonnet teammates plus a lead for 10–15 minutes. Watch it happen on the shared screen; you will build a smaller team yourself in Lab 8.

The demo implements Part 2 of specs/feature-refunds.md: adding a required reason field to NotificationEvent. It is the ideal team job: a contract change that all three services consume, where the workers genuinely need to coordinate. One ownership detail learned from the live run: the schema file edit goes to the lead, because the shipped path_guard hook fences payments-implementer inside services/payments/: hand it contracts/ and the hook blocks the edit. With the flag enabled, the instructor starts from the monorepo root: 

Create an agent team to implement Part 2 of specs/feature-refunds.md.
Spawn three teammates from the payments-implementer, notifications-implementer and
gateway-implementer agent definitions. Partition ownership strictly:
- you, the lead, own contracts/notification.schema.json — apply the approved
  schema diff yourself (path_guard fences the teammates into their services)
- payments-implementer owns services/payments/ and designs the schema change
- notifications-implementer owns services/notifications/
- gateway-implementer owns services/gateway/ and the end-to-end check
The schema task blocks the other tasks. Require plan approval on the
schema task before any code is written.

Three things are worth watching for as the team runs:

  1. Task claiming and unblocking. The teammates claim tasks from the shared list (file locking guarantees no double-claims), and the two service tasks sit blocked until the schema task completes, then unblock on their own.
  2. Coordination through the lead. Peer-to-peer messaging is the documented design, but the shipped implementer definitions allowlist only Read, Grep, Glob, Edit, Write, and Bash (no team tools), so in the captured run every one of the 31 messages was a lead–teammate relay. Watch the lead carry the coordination: payments-implementer surfaces its blockers at the plan gate, the lead approves with a scope cut, concedes a correction (“you were right about path_guard and I was wrong”), and relays the unblocks as each dependency lands. Give the teammate definitions the team tools and you get true peer messages instead.
  3. The lead’s synthesis. When the last task completes, the lead verifies the Part 2 acceptance criteria (schema, payments, and notifications landed as a coherent set) and reports one combined summary.

Lab 8: a small team of your own

Time-boxed: 15 minutes. Pick one tier. Keep the team small, use Sonnet teammates, and shut the team down when you finish.

Easy: a review-only team

Enable the flag, then spawn a team of 2 teammates in review-only mode (no code changes) over the refunds feature you merged earlier in this module. Give each a different lens: for example, contract compliance versus error handling. Read their peer messages: where do the two reviews disagree, and how do they resolve it?

Hard: implement Part 2 with a gated team

Spawn 3 teammates to implement the Part 2 contract change, with strict file-ownership partitioning (each teammate owns a different service’s files: remember, no worktree isolation here). Add a TaskCompleted hook that runs your service tests and exits 2 on failure, and watch it reopen at least one task before the team converges.

You succeeded when: the task list shows claimed and completed tasks, and the lead relayed at least one teammate report. Default teammates (the Easy tier’s, spawned without a restricted agent definition) have the team tools and do message each other peer-to-peer; only teammates spawned from the restricted implementer definitions relay through the lead. If you see true peer messages, note the difference from the relay pattern in Demo 8.
